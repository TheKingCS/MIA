# MIA Self-Development: Autonomous Organization & Dashboard Architecture

Mission

Develop a foundational capability within MIA's Self-Development System that allows MIA to independently understand, organize, structure, configure and continuously improve her own information architecture.

MIA must learn how to organize new developments rather than relying on developers or the user to manually create every category, application, dashboard, relationship and navigation destination.

This is not simply an automatic dashboard generator.

It is an intelligence capability that allows MIA to understand new information and activities, reason about their relationships to existing systems, and determine the most appropriate way to represent and manage them.

The long-term goal is for MIA to become increasingly capable of organizing her own expanding ecosystem.

---

1. Autonomous Organizational Reasoning

MIA must be capable of receiving any new development, subject, project, responsibility, interest, device, system or collection of information and independently determining how it should be organized.

She should reason about:

- What is this development?
- What purpose does it serve?
- What information needs to be maintained?
- What existing environments does it relate to?
- Does it belong inside an existing environment?
- Does it warrant its own subdashboard?
- Does it warrant an entirely new environment?
- What existing applications and capabilities can support it?
- What new capabilities are missing?
- What information should be shared with other dashboards?
- How should it appear in MIA's navigation?
- How should it evolve as additional information becomes available?

MIA must not rely exclusively on predefined categories.

She should understand the organizational hierarchy, but also recognize when a new development doesn't fit existing classifications.

She must be capable of proposing new organizational structures when necessary.

2. Universal Dashboard Configuration

Develop a system that allows MIA to autonomously configure dashboards for virtually any subject or group of related information.

MIA should be able to create dashboards at different levels of complexity.

Examples:

Example A: Beekeeping

MIA recognizes beekeeping as part of Plants & Animals.

She creates a Beekeeping subdashboard containing:

- Hive inventory
- Hive locations
- Inspections
- Colony health
- Feeding records
- Honey production
- Equipment
- Seasonal schedules
- Tasks and reminders
- Related expenses

She integrates relevant information into the larger Plants & Animals dashboard.

Example B: A new business

MIA recognizes a new business venture and determines that it needs a dedicated management dashboard.

Potential components:

- Business overview
- Finances
- Customers
- Orders
- Inventory
- Equipment
- Projects
- Marketing
- Compliance
- Performance metrics

She connects existing financial, calendar, file and communications systems rather than unnecessarily recreating them.

Example C: A new hobby

MIA recognizes a new interest and creates an appropriately lightweight organization.

She might create:

- Learning resources
- Progress tracking
- Equipment inventory
- Notes
- Goals
- Relevant skills and missions

She should not create an unnecessarily complicated management environment for something that only needs a few records.

Example D: A new technology or device

MIA identifies a new device and determines how it should be integrated into her device ecosystem.

She considers:

- Device information
- Connection status
- Configuration
- Capabilities
- Permissions
- Diagnostics
- Related applications
- Automations
- Security

These examples are demonstrations, not predefined limits.

MIA must be able to apply this reasoning to entirely unfamiliar developments.

3. Intelligent Organizational Hierarchy

MIA must understand the difference between:

1. Environments: major areas of life or operation.
2. Dashboards: management and overview interfaces.
3. Subdashboards: specialized departments or subjects.
4. Applications: functional capabilities.
5. Tools: individual operations and utilities.
6. Records: stored information and history.
7. Widgets: individual pieces of information or functionality displayed on dashboards.

She must understand that not everything deserves its own application or dashboard.

For every new development, she should select the smallest organizational structure that adequately serves its purpose while allowing it to grow.

Every application should have a canonical organizational home while supporting contextual access from other relevant environments.

Avoid duplicated applications, duplicated data and competing organizational hierarchies.

4. Autonomous Dashboard Design

MIA should be capable of configuring the actual dashboard presentation, not merely creating a category and placing links inside it.

She should determine which information deserves prominence based on the purpose of the dashboard.

Potential dashboard components include:

- Overview and status
- Important metrics
- Active projects
- Tasks and objectives
- Upcoming events
- Inventory
- Financial information
- Historical records
- Progress tracking
- Alerts and notifications
- Charts and visualizations
- Related applications
- Relevant files and resources
- MIA's observations and recommendations

Not every dashboard needs every component.

MIA should determine which components are appropriate for the subject.

She should also determine the layout, organization, widget selection and information density.

The resulting interface must follow MIA's established visual design language, including her spatial interface principles, while adapting to different screen sizes and devices.

Dashboard configurations should be declarative, reusable and dynamically renderable rather than requiring new hardcoded screens for every new development.

5. Organizational Learning and Self-Development

This capability must integrate directly with MIA's Self-Knowledge, Introspection and Self-Development systems.

MIA should continuously improve her understanding of:

- Her own applications and their capabilities.
- Her existing organizational structure.
- Relationships between systems.
- Available data sources.
- Existing widgets and dashboard components.
- Available automation capabilities.
- Limitations and missing functionality.
- Successful organizational patterns.

When she encounters a new development, she should inspect her existing ecosystem before deciding to create anything new.

She should reuse existing capabilities whenever possible.

When she identifies a missing capability, she should distinguish between:

A. Something she can accomplish using existing functionality.

B. Something she can accomplish by configuring existing components differently.

C. Something requiring a new application, tool or integration.

D. Something requiring actual software development.

E. Something currently impossible because of technical or permission limitations.

She must never pretend that a capability exists simply because she has created a place to display it.

6. Autonomous Organizational Lifecycle

MIA must support the complete lifecycle of organizational development.

Discover → Understand → Classify → Plan → Configure → Integrate → Validate → Learn → Improve

Detailed behavior:

1. Discover new information or a development.
2. Analyze its purpose, scope and relationships.
3. Determine its organizational placement.
4. Inspect existing applications and capabilities.
5. Design the appropriate hierarchy and dashboard.
6. Configure the dashboard and necessary connections.
7. Validate that the resulting functionality actually works.
8. Update her self-knowledge and organizational registry.
9. Monitor how the system is used.
10. Improve the organization when new requirements emerge.

She must also recognize when existing dashboards become too large, cluttered, redundant or difficult to navigate.

She should be capable of reorganizing existing structures without losing information or breaking existing functionality.

7. Autonomy and Approval

Use the existing Self-Development autonomy philosophy:

MIA should be able to independently propose improvements and execute appropriate low-risk organizational changes within her granted permissions.

Establish clear action boundaries.

May autonomously:

- Classify information.
- Propose organizational changes.
- Create dashboard configurations.
- Add existing widgets.
- Organize records and references.
- Establish relationships between information.
- Update her organizational registry.
- Recommend improvements.
- Validate configurations.

Requires approval when appropriate:

- Deleting or permanently moving information.
- Changing permissions or security settings.
- Connecting external accounts.
- Installing new integrations.
- Modifying critical application behavior.
- Creating new executable code or automations with consequential effects.
- Making changes that could disrupt existing workflows.

Every meaningful organizational change should be auditable and reversible wherever technically possible.

MIA should explain her reasoning when making significant structural decisions.

8. Natural Language Interaction

The user should be able to interact with this capability naturally.

Examples:

"Organize everything related to beekeeping."

"Create a dashboard for my new woodworking business."

"Can you organize all my information about solar power?"

"Build me a dashboard for managing the greenhouse."

"Find a better way to organize my projects."

"These applications are getting cluttered. Can you reorganize them?"

"Create a dashboard that brings together everything related to my financial independence."

MIA should understand the request, inspect existing information, determine the appropriate structure and carry out the work within her permissions.

She should not require the user to manually specify every category, widget or relationship.

When essential information is missing, she should ask targeted questions rather than making consequential assumptions.

9. Self-Organization Dashboard

Create an organizational management interface within MIA's Self-Development environment.

This should allow MIA and the user to inspect how MIA has organized her ecosystem.

Include:

- Complete organizational hierarchy.
- All environments and dashboards.
- Application ownership and relationships.
- Dashboard configurations.
- Shared services.
- Widget inventory.
- Organizational dependencies.
- Unclassified information.
- Redundant or overlapping applications.
- Missing organizational capabilities.
- Proposed improvements.
- Recent organizational changes.
- Configuration history and rollback.

MIA should also be able to inspect this structure herself as part of her self-awareness capabilities.

10. Technical Implementation Requirements

Before implementation:

1. Inspect the existing MIA repository.
2. Study the existing Self-Development and Self-Knowledge architecture.
3. Inspect "core/web_areas.py" and "core/web_surfaces.py".
4. Examine Nexus, dashboard rendering, application registries, widget systems and navigation.
5. Identify existing capabilities that can be reused.
6. Determine how this new capability should integrate with the existing architecture.

Do not create a competing organizational registry if an existing system can be extended.

Do not build a second dashboard framework unnecessarily.

Preserve existing application IDs, routes, data relationships and functionality.

Design the organizational engine to be extensible, with clear separation between organizational reasoning, configuration, rendering, validation and execution.

The system should support arbitrary numbers of environments, dashboards, subdashboards, widgets and relationships without requiring manually coded definitions for every new subject.

Add automated tests for:

- New development classification.
- Appropriate dashboard creation.
- Existing capability reuse.
- Cross-environment relationships.
- Dashboard configuration validation.
- Duplicate detection.
- Reorganization.
- Rollback.
- Permission boundaries.
- Unfamiliar or ambiguous subjects.
- Self-knowledge updates.

11. Acceptance Criteria

The implementation should not be considered complete merely because a dashboard can be created through a predefined template.

MIA must demonstrate that she can:

1. Receive a previously unknown subject.
2. Understand its purpose.
3. Determine where it belongs.
4. Identify relevant existing applications and services.
5. Design an appropriate organizational hierarchy.
6. Configure a useful dashboard.
7. Integrate relevant shared capabilities.
8. Explain why she organized it that way.
9. Validate the resulting configuration.
10. Recognize when further development is needed.
11. Learn from the new organizational structure.
12. Reapply the same reasoning to entirely different subjects.

Test this with at least five substantially different examples, including a hobby, a business, a homestead activity, a new device and a complex personal project.

Final Vision

I want MIA to become capable of managing the growth and complexity of her own ecosystem.

As new interests, responsibilities, projects, devices, technologies and capabilities enter our lives, she should be able to understand them and organize the necessary information and functionality intelligently.

I should not have to become the permanent architect of every dashboard and application in MIA.

She should progressively learn how to be her own systems organizer.

This is an important component of her evolution from an assistant that operates predefined applications into an intelligent system that understands, maintains and develops its own operating environment.

Build the underlying organizational intelligence, not just the user interface.
